Overview

This project explores the fundamentals of geometric image transformations, focusing on image warping, homography estimation, and mosaicing. The goal is to register multiple images into a single seamless panorama, illustrating both mathematical and practical challenges in computer vision and computational photography.

Part A.1: Acquiring Images

We capture sets of images with significant overlap and a fixed center of projection (COP) while rotating the camera. Recommended overlap is 40–70% to allow accurate registration. Ideal subjects include indoor scenes, forests, or panoramas.

Part A.3: Image Warping

Once homographies are computed, we warp images to align with a reference frame. Inverse warping is used to avoid holes: for each output pixel, we compute its corresponding source coordinate. Interpolation is then applied to get pixel values. Here we compare nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation methods. We can rectify images by taking things that we know to be rectangles in the source images, and mapping them to rectangle-forming coordinates using a homography.

Interpolation Methods

Nearest-neighbor rounds coordinates to the nearest pixel. It is fast but can produce blocky artifacts. Bilinear interpolation computes a weighted average of the 4 surrounding pixels, producing smoother output at a slight computational cost.

In our experiments, bilinear interpolation produces slightly smoother images, although for large images such as these, the difference is not very noticeable.

Part A.4: Mosaicing

Once the homography H has been computed between the two images, the goal is to map one image (im1) onto the coordinate frame of the other (im2) and blend them into a single seamless mosaic. The first step is to warp im1 using the provided warp_fn (either nearest-neighbor or bilinear interpolation) so that its pixels are mapped according to the homography H. To ensure the final mosaic canvas can hold both images, you compute the bounding coordinates by transforming the corners of im1 with H and combining them with the corners of im2, which allows you to determine the output canvas size and offsets. Next, a blank mosaic canvas is created, and im2 is placed at its proper offset. A mask is also created for im2 to track which pixels are occupied. Similarly, the warped im1 is cropped and aligned to fit within the canvas, and a corresponding mask identifies valid pixels in the warped image. To blend the two images smoothly, the code constructs linear alpha maps for each image based on the distance of each pixel from the center of its nonzero region; pixels near the center have higher alpha values, while those near the edges fade to zero. These alpha maps are then combined and normalized so that overlapping regions are weighted proportionally, avoiding abrupt seams. Finally, the weighted sum of the two images is computed using the alpha maps, producing a smoothly blended mosaic. Any pixel outside both images remains black, while overlapping regions are a linear blend of im1 and im2, resulting in a visually coherent panorama. The final mosaic is clipped to valid pixel values, converted back to the standard RGB ordering, and saved.

Alpha Blending Strategy

  • Weights are 1 at the center of each image, decreasing linearly to 0 at edges.
  • Only overlapping pixels are blended; non-overlapping pixels retain original intensity.

Part 2: Automatic Feature Detection

In the first part of this assignment, we manually chose which points to align in the homography. Now we will perform all of this automatically!

Part B.1: Harris Corner Detection

We begin feature matching by detecting interest points using the Harris Corner Detector. The detector identifies points with strong local intensity variation in both directions. These are potential feature points that are stable under geometric and photometric transformations.

Harris Corner Formula

The Harris matrix is defined as: \[ M = \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \] and the response function is: \[ R = \det(M) - k \cdot (\mathrm{trace}(M))^2 \] where \(k\) is typically between 0.04 and 0.06.

Points with \(R > \tau\) are selected as corners. Non-maximum suppression is then applied to retain only the most salient points.

Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS)

To ensure an even spatial distribution of features, we apply Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS). The algorithm selects a subset of strong corners that are spatially well-separated. For each corner, a suppression radius is computed based on the distance to a stronger corner. The top N points with the largest radii are retained.

ANMS Radius Definition

The suppression radius for corner \(i\) is: \[ r_i = \min_j \|x_i - x_j\| \quad \text{s.t. } R_j > c R_i \] where \(c\) is typically 0.9.

Part B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

Once interest points have been detected, we compute Feature Descriptors for each point. These descriptors capture local image appearance around each corner, enabling robust correspondence between different images.

Descriptor Computation

Around each interest point, we extract a 40×40 pixel window, which is then blurred and downsampled to 8×8. The descriptor is normalized to have zero mean and unit variance: \[ \hat{f} = \frac{f - \mu_f}{\sigma_f} \] where \(f\) is the flattened patch vector.

For simplicity, rotation invariance is not enforced; patches are aligned with the image axes.

Part B.3: Feature Matching

With descriptors computed for each image, we now perform Feature Matching to find correspondences between images. The goal is to identify pairs of points that represent the same physical location in different views.

Nearest Neighbor Matching

For each descriptor \( f_i \) in image 1, we find its two nearest neighbors \( f_j^{(1)} \) and \( f_j^{(2)} \) in image 2 using Euclidean distance. To ensure reliable matches, we apply the Lowe test: \[ \frac{\|f_i - f_j^{(1)}\|}{\|f_i - f_j^{(2)}\|} < \tau \] where \( \tau \approx 0.7 \).

This discards ambiguous matches and keeps only distinctive correspondences.

Part B.4: RANSAC

While descriptor matching yields many correspondences, some are incorrect due to noise or local ambiguities. To robustly estimate the geometric transformation (homography) between images, we apply the RANSAC (RANdom SAmple Consensus) algorithm.

RANSAC repeatedly samples minimal sets of matches, fits a homography, and counts the number of inliers that satisfy: \[ \|x'_i - H x_i\| < \epsilon \] The model with the largest consensus set is retained.

Once robust homographies are computed, we can align all images into a single reference frame and blend them to form a mosaic.

In general, there doesn't seem to be any noticeable quality decrease from the automatic feature detection showing that our algorithms are fairly robust. One drawback of the automatic feature detection is that it requires some amount of hyperparameter tuning in order to generate good results. For all of my images, the same or very similar parameters yielded good results though, so it's possible that these parameters are robust to variations in images.
